Applying to PhD programs is a long process with many moving parts: finding advisors whose work fits, writing a statement of purpose, lining up recommendation letters, and deciding where to apply. These are my notes on the process.

Two documents shaped most of how I think about it:

- [**Applying to Ph.D. Programs in Computer Science**](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~harchol/gradschooltalk.pdf) by [Mor Harchol-Balter](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~harchol/) (CMU). A 22-page guide from someone who has served on PhD admissions at CMU, Berkeley, and MIT.
- [**Prospective Students and Postdocs**](https://ebiyik.github.io/prospective/) by [Erdem Bıyık](https://ebiyik.github.io/) (USC, [Lira Lab](https://liralab.usc.edu/)). One professor's FAQ on how to approach the lab, and how not to.

Read together, they make one thing clear: **every professor wants something different.** One wants you to come to office hours. Another asks you not to email at all. The first step with any lab is finding out what _that_ professor has asked for.

> **Last checked: October 24, 2026.** The CMU guide was last updated in 2014, so its numbers (stipends, GRE, rankings) are dated. The advice on research, letters, and statements still holds up.

## The two references at a glance

## Why a PhD

<!-- Your own reason: what you want out of it, and why now -->

The CMU guide is blunt about what a PhD is: _"a long, in depth research exploration of one topic."_ That means about six years, few courses, and by the end, one narrow problem you know better than anyone. Research is different from classes in ways that catch people off guard:

- Problems don't come with known answers, and nobody hands them to you. Finding a good problem is part of the job.
- There are no grades. You are expected to be self-motivated.
- You work side by side with your advisor, usually for about an hour a week, so you have to plan how to use that time.

Two lines from the guide stuck with me. First: _"Do not even think of applying for a Ph.D. if you have not tried research and/or teaching and found that you like at least one of those."_ Second, a footnote noting that _"you need to be smart"_ is deliberately missing from its list of what a PhD takes. Perseverance and knowing why you want it matter more. If you're unsure, the guide recommends working in a research lab for a few years first.

## Read each professor's page first

Here the two references differ most. The CMU guide's advice for undergraduates looking for research is to go to a professor's office hours or email for a meeting, bring a one-page summary sheet, and expect to try ten times before someone says yes. That is how to start research at your own school.

PhD admissions are a different situation, and Bıyık's page shows how one professor handles it:

- **No PhD inquiry emails.** Bıyık answered every one until December 2024, but was getting 50–100 a month, and most were copy-paste. The page answers the usual questions instead.
- **No fit meetings.** A 30-minute call with every applicant would take almost a month. The lab's research and publication pages are meant to answer the fit question, and fit can't be judged until the whole applicant pool is in.
- **Department doesn't matter.** Bıyık has appointments in both CS and ECE and reviews applications the same way in both. Choose based on the _other_ professors you'd want to work with, and don't try to game which department is easier.
- **No pre-evaluation.** Compare yourself to the lab's current PhD students as they were when they applied. If your application looks similar or stronger, apply.
- **No reminders.** Every application that lists Bıyık as a potential advisor gets reviewed. No interview request in the first 2–3 weeks of January means either your letters aren't complete or there won't be an offer. Another professor may still reach out.
- **Silence is an answer.** For self-funded or remote internships, _"If I do not respond, you can assume it is a negative response."_

Other professors do want a short, specific email before you apply. Some say on their website that they aren't taking students this year. Before you contact anyone, check:

1. Is there a "prospective students" page or FAQ? Read all of it.
2. Are they recruiting for your cycle, and how many students? (Bıyık plans to take 1 for Fall 2027.)
3. Which department(s) can they admit from?
4. Do they want an email, a mention in your statement, or just your application through the portal?

## Choosing labs and advisors

Most of this comes from the CMU guide's section on choosing a program, but it works just as well for building your list:

- **Count the faculty in your area**, minus anyone listed on the website who is actually somewhere else. Then repeat the count for two more areas, since about half of students end up changing their research area.
- **Count the faculty whose work excites you**, minus those not taking students. That's your real pool of possible advisors.
- **Talk to current students.** How often do they meet their advisor? Are they excited about their work? The guide's line is _"Graduate students will reveal all!"_
- **Ask how funding works.** Can you only work with advisors who have grants? If your advisor loses funding, do you end up TAing every semester?
- **Rankings matter mostly for your peers.** They're who you'll learn the most from. The guide also warns that _"a famous professor does not imply a great advisor."_
- **Consider cost of living.** The same stipend goes much further in some cities than others, and that adds up over six years.

On how many schools to apply to, the guide's rule of thumb is about 3 at your level, 2 above it, and 1–2 below it. Also consider schools that are strong in your specific area even if they rank lower overall. Bıyık's page gives one more way to judge your level for a specific lab: look at the current students.

## Research experience

For top programs, the CMU guide says research experience matters most, along with what your letters and statement say about it. Some details:

- You don't need a publication or even a finished result. The committee just needs confidence that _"you know what doing research is like."_
- 4.0 GPA applicants with no research go into the "high risk pile" and get rejected.
- Grades around 3.5/4.0 in CS, math, and engineering are fine, and are judged against your school. Time spent on research beats time spent pushing a 3.5 toward 4.0.
- The GRE counted for little at top schools even in 2014, and many programs have since dropped it. Check each program's requirements.
- For international applicants: publish in venues the committee will recognize, and put English versions of your papers on a personal website.

Where to get experience: research with a professor at your school, summer programs like REUs (USC lists REU, SURE, and others on Bıyık's page), research jobs in industry, an MS project, or reading conference proceedings on your own and trying to improve on a paper.

## Statement of purpose

The CMU guide's main point: the "personal statement" is really a **research statement**. Committees want to know what research you've done, what you want to do, and why you like research. Its template:

1. **Paragraph 1:** The research areas that interest you, and why. This helps route your file to the right faculty.
2. **Paragraphs 2–4:** Projects you worked on. What was the problem? Why did it matter? What did you try? What did you learn? Saying you didn't fully solve it is fine.
3. **Paragraph 5:** Why you need a PhD in particular.
4. **Paragraph 6:** Why this school. Name the people you'd work with and the papers of theirs you enjoyed reading.

This isn't a commitment: about a third of students end up working in a different area. The guide also names two common mistakes:

- **The grade regurgitator** lists rankings and test scores the committee has already seen elsewhere in the application.
- **The boy genius** tells childhood stories (taking apart the family computer at age 3). _"We simply don't care what you did as a child, and we don't believe you either."_

That last paragraph matters even more given pages like Bıyık's. If a professor reviews every application that lists them as a potential advisor, the statement and the advisor field are how they find you. That is the "contact" they've asked for.

## Recommendation letters

The CMU guide calls letters _"perhaps the most important part of your application."_ You usually need three.

**What makes a letter count.** A letter that says you got an A+ and never missed class is marked **D.W.I.C.** ("Did Well In Class") and counts as zero, because your transcript already shows that. A strong letter describes you doing research on your own initiative. Committees circle words like _self-motivated_, _strong research potential_, _own initiative_, _independent_, and _driven_.

**Whom to ask.** People who have seen you do research. Letters from professors count most, then research scientists, then lecturers, employers, and postdocs. Don't ask a grad student. If one supervised you, ask their professor to co-write the letter. A letter carries more weight when the committee knows the writer and the writer can compare you to current PhD students.

**How to ask.** Ask: _"Do you feel comfortable writing a strong letter of recommendation for me to graduate school?"_ This gives them an easy way to say no, which is better than a weak letter. Then give each writer a packet:

- Your statement of purpose
- A summary of every research project, with whom, plus any papers or reports
- Your math, CS, engineering, and science courses, with professors and grades
- Awards and extracurriculars
- On top: a recent photo, a due date **earlier** than the real one, and a note that you'll send a reminder if you haven't heard back by then

Confirm with each school that the letters arrived, and send a thank-you. Bıyık's page shows why checking matters: incomplete letters are one of the two reasons given for not getting an interview request in January.

## Funding and fellowships

Most CS PhD programs cover tuition and pay a stipend, through a research assistantship (your advisor's grants) or a teaching assistantship. The CMU guide's warning: at schools without guaranteed funding, you may be limited to advisors who have grants, or end up TAing for years.

Apply for outside fellowships even before you pick schools. They pay for several years, let you choose any advisor, and look good on an application. The guide lists the NSF GRFP, NDSEG, and Hertz fellowships. Many require US citizenship or permanent residency, and the rules may have changed since 2014. Fellowship deadlines are usually **one to two months before** school deadlines.

Application fees add up too. Bıyık's page points out that professors can't waive them. Fee waivers go through the university, so check each school's eligibility rules.

## Timeline

Put together from both references. Exact deadlines vary by school, so check each one.

| When                               | What                                                                                                     |
| ---------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Well before you apply              | Get research experience. Keep in touch with the professors who will write your letters                   |
| Summer / early fall                | Build your list. Read every professor's prospective-students page. Ask for letters and hand over packets |
| 1–2 months before school deadlines | Fellowship applications are due                                                                          |
| School deadlines                   | Submit applications. Confirm every letter arrived                                                        |
| First 2–3 weeks of January         | Interview requests (Bıyık's timing; other labs differ)                                                   |
| By March                           | Decisions arrive. If you haven't heard, email the admissions contact                                     |
| March–April                        | Visit days. The CMU guide says _"Do not skip these visits."_                                             |
| April 15                           | The usual deadline to accept an offer                                                                    |
