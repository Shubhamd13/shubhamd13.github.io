This guide covers how to apply to a robotics PhD program: when to start research, which program to apply to, how to approach professors, what goes in a statement of purpose, how to get strong letters, and the timeline. It draws on my own applications and on what professors and robotics programs have written about how they admit students.

Robotics PhD students are admitted through robotics programs and through CS, ECE, and mechanical engineering departments, so much of the general CS advice applies. The main general source is Mor Harchol-Balter's [Applying to Ph.D. Programs in Computer Science](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~harchol/gradschooltalk.pdf) (CMU), written by someone who has served on PhD admissions at CMU, Berkeley, and MIT. Robotics professors still point applicants to it, including [Joydeep Biswas](https://www.joydeepb.com/prospective-students.html) at UT Austin. The robotics-specific advice comes from program pages and from robotics professors' pages for prospective students. Where sources disagree, I describe each view. All of them are listed in [Links](#links) at the end.

> The CMU guide was last updated in 2014, so its numbers on stipends, the GRE, and rankings are dated. Its advice on research, letters, and statements still holds. Professors' recruiting plans change every year, so check their pages yourself. If something here is out of date, [email me](mailto:sderh001@ucr.edu) and I'll update it.

## Start research early

You apply to a PhD about a year before you start it. Applications are usually due in December or January, and the program begins the following fall. By the time you apply, you need research experience, professors who can write about it, and some confidence that you want to spend five or six more years doing research.

That last part is easy to underestimate. The CMU guide says: _"Do not even think of applying for a Ph.D. if you have not tried research and/or teaching and found that you like at least one of those."_ Knowing that you enjoy going deep into one problem usually comes only after you have done research for a while. Because applications go out a year ahead, you need to know this a year early.

For many master's students, the timing doesn't work out. I did my MS at UC Riverside, which runs on ten-week quarters. As an international student, my priority was to finish quickly, mostly for financial reasons, and I completed the degree in one year and three months, including a three-month summer break. In a program that short, PhD deadlines arrive while you are still taking classes. The final months often go to chasing a first publication, working day and night on a project, and applying to jobs as a backup. The realization that you want to do research may come near the end of the degree, when the deadlines are close or already past.

My advice is to start research in your first term, even in a small way. A few months of real research before your final term gives you something to write about, a professor who can write your letter, and time to find out whether you want a PhD at all.

To find a research position at your own school, the CMU guide suggests going to a professor's office hours or emailing for a meeting, bringing a one-page summary of your background and interests, and expecting to ask several professors before one says yes. In robotics, hands-on work also counts. [Duke Robotics](https://robotics.duke.edu/students/prospective) points master's students who want research toward an independent study with a faculty member, which can grow into a thesis, and toward its robotics club, which competes in RoboSub.

Some labs suggest specific projects. For navigation, Xuesu Xiao's [RobotiXX lab](https://people.cs.gmu.edu/~xiao/RobotiXX/opportunities.html) at George Mason says one way to stand out is to set up the simulation environments of The BARN Challenge, a navigation competition, and build a method that performs well, preferably better than the baselines. A result like that is something you can finish during a short master's, and it gives you a concrete project to write about.

If you reach the deadlines without enough research, waiting a year is a real option. Tim Dettmers [writes](https://timdettmers.com/2018/11/26/phd-applications/) about extending a master's by a year to do research internships, and credits those internships for the PhD admissions that followed. The CMU guide suggests working in a research lab for a few years if you are unsure. [Sonia Roberts on Robohub](https://robohub.org/careers-in-robotics-should-you-get-a-phd-or-go-into-industry) argues that a few years in industry can build savings, show you whether you really want a PhD, and make you a stronger applicant.

Some labs also offer a path from a master's into the PhD. At RobotiXX, master's students first join projects led by senior students, with the hope of continuing to a doctorate, and assistantships can open up after one or two semesters of strong research. Aniket Bera's [IDEAS Lab](https://cs.purdue.edu/corporate/employment/ideaslab.html) at Purdue says exceptional BS and MS students may be considered for the PhD program based on their performance and research fit.

## What a PhD involves

<!-- Your own reason: what you want out of it, and why now -->

The CMU guide describes a PhD as _"a long, in depth research exploration of one topic."_ That means about six years, few courses, and by the end, one narrow problem you know better than anyone. Research differs from classes in a few ways. Problems don't come with known answers, and finding a good problem is part of the job. There are no grades, so you have to motivate yourself. You meet your advisor for about an hour a week, so you have to plan how to use that time.

The guide's list of what a PhD takes deliberately leaves out being smart. Perseverance and a clear reason for wanting the degree matter more.

The [Robohub article](https://robohub.org/careers-in-robotics-should-you-get-a-phd-or-go-into-industry) compares a robotics PhD with going straight into industry, which is worth reading if you are applying to both:

- A PhD works more like a job than more school. You set your own research questions, while industry roles usually come with smaller, well-defined goals and a team.
- Academic research can have a large impact, but rarely an immediate one. The article names frustration with slow, invisible impact as a major source of burnout.
- The pay gap is large. Its example compares a $100,000 entry-level job with a $40,000 stipend over six years, which comes to roughly $400,000 in lost earnings once raises are included.
- A PhD is required for some jobs, mostly in academia. In industry it is often treated as roughly equal to a few years of work experience.

The article's conclusion is that a PhD makes sense if you enjoy open-ended, self-directed research or want the specific jobs it leads to, not for prestige.

## Which program to apply to

Robotics faculty sit in different departments, and the way you apply depends on the school:

- **A robotics PhD through a home school.** At [Georgia Tech](https://research.gatech.edu/robotics/phd-program-robotics), you apply to the Robotics PhD through one of five home schools: Interactive Computing, Aerospace, Biomedical, Electrical and Computer, or Mechanical Engineering. Your home school also sets some requirements, such as a teaching practicum. In the [College of Computing](https://www.cc.gatech.edu/degree-programs/phd-robotics), the GRE is optional for the Robotics PhD.
- **A department, chosen with your advisor.** [Duke Robotics](https://robotics.duke.edu/students/prospective) advises PhD applicants to contact the faculty member they want to work with before applying. If that professor is taking students and sees a fit, they will say which department to apply to: mechanical engineering, ECE, or CS.
- **Any of several departments, as long as you name the professor.** Michael Posa's [DAIR Lab](https://dair.engineering.upenn.edu/joining/) at Penn takes students through mechanical engineering (MEAM), ESE, or CIS, and asks you to name Posa in your application. Erdem Bıyık [at USC](https://ebiyik.github.io/prospective/) reviews applications the same way in CS and ECE, and advises choosing based on the other faculty you would want to work with. Katie Driggs-Campbell [at UIUC](https://krdc.web.illinois.edu/) takes students through ECE or CS.
- **One department.** Tom Silver's [lab at Princeton](https://prpl-group.com/#join) takes students through ECE. Francesco Bullo [at UCSB](https://fbullo.github.io/msg-prospective.html) asks applicants to apply to mechanical engineering or a related department. At UT Austin, Biswas takes students through the CS PhD.

Before applying, find out which programs each of your potential advisors can admit through. Then pick the one whose courses and qualifying exams suit you best.

## What robotics labs look for

"Robotics" covers very different work, and labs ask for different backgrounds. Compare four pages:

- Junaed Sattar's [lab at Minnesota](https://junaedsattar.cs.umn.edu/prospective-students) tests its research on physical robots, often outdoors, so it wants strong coding skills and an interest in robots themselves. Hardware experience with microcontrollers, sensors, and actuators is a plus. Applicants mainly interested in computer vision, deep learning, or language, without a strong interest in robotics, are unlikely to fit.
- Seyed Amir Tafrishi [at Cardiff](https://sites.google.com/site/samirtafrishi/Applications) warns that robotics involves heavy mathematics, drawing on control theory, mechanics, electronics, and materials, not just AI. Applicants without an engineering background or robotics and controls coursework are unlikely to be considered.
- Bullo values strong mathematical preparation over the specific undergraduate major.
- AJung Moon [at McGill](https://sites.google.com/view/ajungmoon/prospective-students), who works on robot ethics and human-robot interaction, welcomes backgrounds in policy, ethics, and psychology as well as engineering. Moon lists teamwork and curiosity among the main qualities sought, and says grades are not the first or only thing considered.

Labs within a single subfield differ in the same way. In social robot navigation, Biswas looks for a strong mathematical background from any major, with a focus on _"solving problems, not promoting tools,"_ and deploys the lab's work on physical robots. Bera's IDEAS Lab instead asks for computer vision and machine learning, especially detecting, tracking, and predicting the motion of people. I compared 20 social navigation labs in a separate post, [Social Navigation PhD Labs]({% post_url 2026-10-28-social-navigation-phd-labs %}).

So "robotics" alone doesn't describe what you want to do. Tafrishi asks applicants not to write vague statements like "AI" or "robotics." Name the problem, such as navigation in crowds, manipulation, or legged locomotion, and the methods you have used.

Two programs publish how their faculty judge applications. [Oregon State Robotics](https://engineering.oregonstate.edu/academics/programs/robotics/graduate/graduate-application-advice) lists five criteria: academic preparation, research experience, extracurricular activities that show drive and collaboration, work that makes robotics and STEM more inclusive (outreach, tutoring, mentoring), and alignment with the program, for which you name 2–3 faculty you would like as advisors. At [Michigan Robotics](https://robotics.umich.edu/news/2024/what-are-specific-faculty-looking-for-in-phd-applications), twelve faculty each answered the question in their own words, and they don't all agree:

- **Publications.** Yulun Tian looks for publications or substantial projects, and Mark Draelos for sustained academic excellence in courses, projects, or publications. Jessy Grizzle, by contrast, looks at applicants from smaller schools for class projects they pushed beyond what was required.
- **GPA.** Grizzle says a perfect GPA isn't necessary, and Yanran Ding values technical depth and a clear story over a perfect GPA. Anouck Girard asks for evidence of excellent scholarship.
- **Personality versus technical depth.** Brent Gillespie wants to hear a distinct personality in the essays. Talia Moore wants to know what gaps you see in the field and how you would address them.
- **Independence.** Lionel Robert looks for the shift from a strong student to an independent scholar who can frame problems and handle negative feedback.

What they share is a preference for specific, detailed descriptions of your own projects: your role, what went wrong, and what you learned. Patricia Alves-Oliveira asks applicants to go beyond buzzwords.

## Contacting professors

Professors disagree most about whether you should email them before applying. Read each professor's page before you write to anyone. The sources here range from "please don't" to "please do":

- **Don't email; apply through the portal.** Bıyık stopped answering PhD inquiry emails after receiving 50–100 a month, and reviews every application that lists Bıyık as a potential advisor. Sattar considers only applications submitted through the university's admissions system, and ignores emails about research unrelated to the lab. Posa writes that the lab can't respond to all unsolicited emails. Justin Hart [at UT Austin](https://justinhart.net/joining/) does not evaluate candidates by email or give feedback outside the formal admissions process.
- **You can email, but don't expect a reply.** Driggs-Campbell invites prospective students to email about openings, but says a reply is unlikely because of the volume, and encourages applying through ECE or CS and listing Driggs-Campbell as a faculty member of interest.
- **No need to email, but you can.** Silver's lab page says there is no need to email ahead of time, although you are welcome to. Marynel Vázquez [at Yale](https://www.marynel.net/open_positions/) asks PhD applicants to apply and name Vázquez in the application.
- **Fill in the lab's form.** A growing number of labs use an interest form instead of email. Michael Everett's [lab at Northeastern](https://neu-autonomy.github.io/lab_website/joinus) says the form is not an application, but it lets the lab look at your background when emails can't all be answered. You still apply through the university.
- **Email after you apply.** Bullo asks you to study the group's papers first, apply, and then send one email saying you have applied. Bullo asks people not to email repeatedly and is unlikely to offer funding before knowing a student well or getting a strong recommendation from someone Bullo knows. Andrea Bajcsy's [lab at CMU](https://cmu-intentlab.github.io/join.html) is less likely to reply to emails sent before the deadline, and asks for one after you have applied, with "Prospective PhD Student" in the subject line.
- **Email your materials along with your application.** Xiao asks PhD applicants to apply to George Mason and also email a single PDF with a cover letter, CV, transcript, references, and publications, under a set subject line.
- **Email first.** Duke Robotics strongly advises contacting advisors in advance. At Michigan, Steven Ceron asks applicants to email a CV. Moon's process at McGill starts with an email, with a set subject line: "(Domestic/International) Prospective Student for (start date) (PhD/M.Sc.) -- (Your Name)". In it, Moon asks you to name a paper of the lab's that interested you and ask a question it raised, describe your research experience, and attach a CV and a writing sample. Tafrishi, at a UK university, asks for a CV, transcripts, up to 2–3 papers, and a short research proposal, and also gives a subject-line format. Takayuki Kanda's [HRI lab at Kyoto University](https://www.robot.soc.i.kyoto-u.ac.jp/en/join-us/) asks doctoral applicants to get in touch before applying, to discuss their research background and interests.

In these examples, the professors who ask for an email first are mostly outside the US, in Canada, the UK, and Japan, or in departments where the advisor picks students directly. Not every country outside the US works this way: Harold Soh's [lab at NUS](https://clear-nus.github.io/about) in Singapore defers to the department's admissions committee. Dettmers notes the same broad pattern: emailing first is not required in the US but sometimes is in Europe. Dettmers also says that cold emails without a personal connection rarely get read, and that contact works best when one of your letter writers introduces you.

Before reaching out, check:

1. Is there a "prospective students" page or FAQ? Read all of it.
2. Are they recruiting for your cycle, and for which start term? Moon, for example, is not taking students for Fall 2026 or Winter 2027, and Bullo is unlikely to have a position for 2027/28. Jiachen Li's [lab at Georgia Tech](https://tasl-lab.github.io/join/), on the other hand, is looking for several fully funded PhD students starting in Spring or Fall 2027.
3. Which departments can they admit students through?
4. Do they want an email, a mention in your statement, or just your application through the portal? If they give a subject-line format, use it exactly.

If you do email, be specific. A CV and GPA alone don't say why you fit. Moon notes that a high GPA doesn't show research or teamwork, and Sattar notes that a CV and publication list may not be enough to judge fit.

### Example: social navigation labs

My own research interest is social robot navigation, the topic of my [post on simulators]({% post_url 2026-10-07-social-robot-navigation %}). Among the 20 labs I looked at in that area, every approach above shows up: Hart asks for no email, Driggs-Campbell welcomes one but probably won't reply, Everett uses a form, Bajcsy wants an email only after you apply, Xiao wants a PDF with a set subject line, and Kanda wants contact first. ## Choosing where to apply

Most of this comes from the CMU guide's section on choosing a program:

- Count the faculty in your area, leaving out anyone listed on the website who has moved elsewhere. Do the same for two other areas you might enjoy, since about half of students change their research area.
- Count the faculty whose work excites you and who are taking students. That is your real pool of possible advisors. Dettmers gives the same advice: choose universities mainly by possible advisors, and prefer programs with more than one.
- Talk to current students, and former ones too. Ask how often they meet their advisor and whether they are excited about their work. Chaitanya Joshi [points out](https://web.archive.org/web/20231203175425/https://www.chaitjo.com/post/phd-applications/) that a professor who seems friendly on social media may run a very different lab, and that you are picking the advisor as much as they are picking you. At Michigan, Ram Vasudevan also encourages applicants to contact the lab's current and former students.
- Ask how funding works. At some schools you can only work with advisors who have grants, and if your advisor loses funding you may end up as a teaching assistant every term.
- Rankings matter mostly for your peers, who are the people you will learn the most from. A famous professor is not necessarily a good advisor.
- Consider cost of living. The same stipend goes much further in some cities, and the difference adds up over six years.

On how many schools to apply to, the sources differ. The CMU guide suggests about three schools at your level, two above it, and one or two below it, around six or seven in total. Dettmers suggests 10–15, including one or two where admission is very likely, and warns that more than that leaves too little time to polish each application. Application fees and the time each statement takes are the real limits.

Include schools that are strong in your specific area even if they rank lower overall. To judge your level for a particular lab, look at its current PhD students and what they had done when they applied.

## What committees look for

For top programs, research experience matters most, along with what your letters and statement say about it. How much a publication matters is where sources differ:

- The CMU guide says you don't need a publication or a finished result. The committee needs to see that you know what doing research is like. Applicants with a 4.0 GPA and no research are considered high risk and are usually rejected.
- Dettmers ranks recommendation letters above publications.
- Bullo lists publications in international venues, a degree from a top school, and letters from internationally known professors among the things that help. Hart looks for publications at ICRA, IROS, HRI, or RO-MAN, and Xiao strongly prefers a good publication record.
- At Michigan, as above, some faculty look for publications and others accept strong class projects, especially from smaller schools.

On grades, most sources agree that past a certain point they stop mattering. The CMU guide says around 3.5/4.0 in CS, math, and engineering is fine, judged relative to your school, and that time on research is worth more than raising a 3.5 to a 4.0. Dettmers says anything above 3.5 doesn't matter and treats the GRE as a filter. Many programs, including Georgia Tech's Robotics PhD, no longer require the GRE.

International applicants should publish in venues the committee will recognize and put English versions of their papers on a personal website.

Research experience can come from a professor at your school, a summer program such as an REU, a research job in industry, an MS project or thesis, robotics competitions, or reading recent conference papers on your own and trying to improve on one.

## Statement of purpose

Despite the name, the statement of purpose is a research statement. The committee wants to know what research you have done, what you want to do next, and why you like research. The CMU guide suggests this structure:

1. **Paragraph 1:** The research areas that interest you, and why. This helps route your application to the right faculty.
2. **Paragraphs 2–4:** The projects you worked on. What was the problem, why did it matter, what did you try, and what did you learn? It is fine to say you didn't fully solve it.
3. **Paragraph 5:** Why you need a PhD in particular.
4. **Paragraph 6:** Why this school. Name the faculty you would like to work with and papers of theirs you have read.

The [Stanford CS advice](https://www.cs.stanford.edu/sites/g/files/sbiybj28076/files/media/file/general-advice-for-cs-phd-applicants.pdf), collected from current PhD students, describes a similar shape: an introduction that names your area and summarizes your research experience in one sentence, body paragraphs that each tell one focused story about a project, and a final paragraph that is specific to the school. Only that last paragraph usually changes between schools. Its other tips:

- Statements are commonly about two pages. They run long when they go too deep into technical details or repeat the whole CV.
- For each project, explain why the problem was worth solving, what you personally contributed, what you learned, and what didn't work off the shelf and why.
- Use the active voice ("I built," "we published"), and consider short bold headers to label each project.

Some programs ask for two essays. [Michigan Robotics](https://robotics.umich.edu/academics/graduate/admissions/academic-and-personal-statements/) wants an academic statement (about a page that goes deep on your past research, then half a page on future interests and the faculty whose work fits them) and a separate personal statement about your path and motivation. It asks you not to summarize a paper's abstract, and to split any overlapping topic so the two essays don't repeat each other.

Naming faculty is expected almost everywhere. Oregon State asks for 2–3 names, and Bullo, Posa, Silver, and Vázquez all ask to be named. At Michigan, Grizzle says naming faculty is not essential. Naming an area is not a commitment either: about a third of students end up working on something else.

### How to open

Andy Pavlo (CMU) wrote a short post on [how to write a bad statement](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~pavlo/blog/2015/10/how-to-write-a-bad-statement-for-a-computer-science-phd-admissions-application.html), built from openings read on the admissions committee. The most common is the **boy genius**: a story about how early you fell in love with computers. Pavlo sorts real examples by age, from _"When I was 6, my father bought a computer for me"_ through middle school and high school. Pavlo credits Harchol-Balter with the earliest one: _"When I was born, my mother gave me a glass ball to play with."_ The CMU guide's verdict on these is blunt: _"We simply don't care what you did as a child, and we don't believe you either."_ The Stanford advice gives a similar example to avoid: _"Ever since I was a child, I have been interested in unraveling the mysteries of intelligence…"_

The college version is the class epiphany: a course you took made you want a PhD. Pavlo's point is that this shows what you sat through, not what you did. The CMU guide adds one more mistake: repeating grades and test scores that the committee can already see elsewhere in the application.

The sources differ on how much style the opening needs. Pavlo would rather read a plain statement of your research interests, even a dry one, than an irrelevant story. Dettmers calls the first paragraph the most important part and recommends a hook that makes the reader want to continue. Both start with your research interests. If you use a hook, make it about something you built or a problem you worked on, such as a research project, a paper, an open-source tool, or a robot.

### Write it yourself

At Michigan, Gillespie writes that essays which seem AI-generated are an immediate turn-off, and several faculty there ask for a genuine voice. Faculty read hundreds of statements, and generic phrasing stands out. Use tools to check grammar if you like, but the ideas and the voice should be yours.

### Get feedback

Ask your professors, labmates, and other applicants to read drafts, as Joshi recommends. Some schools have students who review applications before they are submitted. At CMU, the student-run [Robotics Institute Graduate Application Support Program](https://ri-gasp.github.io) matches PhD and MSR applicants with a current RI student who gives feedback on their materials. Spots go first-come, first-served, so sign up early.

### Your CV

The Stanford advice also covers the CV. Keep it to one or two pages, with education, research experience, and publications on the first page. A short publications list is fine, and papers under review can be listed if they are clearly labeled. Start each bullet with a strong verb, and keep bullets to about one line. Tafrishi suggests including robotics competitions, conference attendance, documented robot projects, and robotics or controls courses.

## Recommendation letters

The CMU guide calls letters _"perhaps the most important part of your application,"_ and Dettmers agrees, ranking them above publications. Most programs ask for three.

A letter that only says you got an A and never missed class adds nothing, because your transcript already shows that. A strong letter describes you doing research and taking initiative. Words like _self-motivated_, _independent_, and _strong research potential_ carry weight. Several Michigan faculty also look for letters that show you working both independently and in a team. Dettmers suggests choosing writers who highlight different strengths, rather than three letters that say the same thing.

Ask people who have seen you do research. Letters from professors count most, followed by research scientists, then lecturers, employers, and postdocs. Avoid letters from graduate students. If a graduate student supervised you, ask their professor to co-write the letter. A letter carries more weight when the committee knows the writer and the writer can compare you to current PhD students.

When you ask, phrase it as: _"Do you feel comfortable writing a strong letter of recommendation for me to graduate school?"_ This gives them an easy way to decline, which is better than a weak letter. Then send each writer:

- Your statement of purpose
- A summary of each research project, who you worked with, and any papers or reports
- Your math, CS, engineering, and science courses, with professors and grades
- Awards and activities outside class
- A due date a little earlier than the real one, and a note that you will send a reminder

Confirm with each school that every letter arrived, and thank your writers afterward. Missing letters can keep your application from being reviewed in time.

## Funding and fellowships

Most US robotics and CS PhD programs cover tuition and pay a stipend through a research assistantship, funded by your advisor's grants, or a teaching assistantship. At schools without guaranteed funding, your choice of advisor may be limited to those with grants.

Funding also affects deadlines. Sattar's lab and Oregon State both use December 15 as the deadline for funding consideration, and later applications may be reviewed only after that pool. Moon notes that grant funding for a September start often isn't clear until early May, so silence before then may not be a no.

Apply for outside fellowships, such as the NSF GRFP, NDSEG, and Hertz, before you have chosen schools. They fund several years, let you work with any advisor, and strengthen your application. Many require US citizenship or permanent residency, so check the current rules. Outside the US, mention scholarships you have won or applied for. Moon asks for these in the first email, and Tafrishi asks for the scholarship name in the subject line. Fellowship deadlines are usually one to two months before school deadlines.

A fellowship can also make a lab more willing to take you. Bajcsy's page says students who are applying for or already hold an external fellowship are of particular interest, because self-funded students make it easier to find a spot in the lab. In Japan, Kanda's lab hosts only MEXT scholars recommended by the Japanese embassy in their home country, so international applicants apply through the embassy first and contact the lab after passing its screening.

Application fees add up across many schools. Fee waivers are handled by the university, not by individual professors, so check each school's eligibility rules.

## Timeline

Exact dates vary by school, so check each one. This table follows the usual US calendar. Some labs also admit students in the spring, such as Li's lab at Georgia Tech. Outside the US the calendar can be entirely different: Kyoto University holds entrance exams in August for April enrollment and in February for October enrollment.

| When                                 | What                                                                                                                                   |
| ------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| First term of your degree            | Start research. Get to know the professors who might write your letters                                                                |
| Summer / early fall before you apply | Build your list of schools and advisors. Read each professor's prospective-students page. Ask for letters. Sign up for review programs |
| 1–2 months before school deadlines   | Submit fellowship applications                                                                                                         |
| December–January                     | Submit applications. Many funding deadlines are around December 15. Confirm every letter arrived                                       |
| January–February                     | Interviews with interested faculty                                                                                                     |
| By March                             | Decisions arrive. If you haven't heard, email the admissions contact                                                                   |
| March–April                          | Visit days. Go if you can; talking to current students is the best way to judge a lab                                                  |
| April 15                             | The usual deadline to accept an offer at US schools                                                                                    |
